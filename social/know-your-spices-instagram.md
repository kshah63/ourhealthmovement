Ready-to-post carousel copy for the @our_healthmovement Instagram, one post per
spice, mirroring the guides on the website. Everything below is written to be
copied and pasted straight in. Each spice has: **carousel slides** (one screen
each), a **caption**, and **hashtags**.

## How to use this

- **Post order:** run them in the same order as the guides (turmeric first). One
  or two a week keeps the series feeling steady without flooding the feed.
- **Design:** keep the earthy brand look, cream background (#f4ead6), terracotta
  headings (#b4552f), Fraunces for the big words, Hanken for the body. A small
  fern mark in the corner ties every slide back to the site. Slide 1 is the
  hook, the last slide is always the call to action.
- **Caption:** the first line is the hook that shows before "more", so keep it
  strong. No em dashes anywhere, in keeping with the site.
- **Hashtags:** a **core set** to reuse, plus a **spice set** unique to each
  post. Instagram can flag the exact same block of tags on every post, so rotate
  the core set (drop three or four, swap in others) rather than pasting it
  identically every time.

  ourhealthmovement.co.uk/guides/spices.

## 01 · Turmeric

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 01
**Turmeric**
The golden root that has been food and medicine for thousands of years.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Turmeric has been used in India for thousands of years as both food and
medicine. Its golden pigment, curcumin, is what modern research keeps circling
back to for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity. In Ayurveda it is
prized for kindling agni, your digestive fire.

**Slide 3**
The golden rule: add black pepper
Piperine, the compound that makes pepper sharp, can dramatically increase how
much curcumin your body absorbs. A pinch is all it takes.

**Slide 4**
And a little fat
Curcumin is fat soluble, so turmeric does its best work cooked in oil, ghee or a
milk of some kind. Warm it gently to deepen both flavour and, traditionally, its
benefits.

**Slide 5**
Bring it into the day
Golden milk before bed. A teaspoon stirred into dal and soups. Tossed over
cauliflower or root veg with cumin and salt before roasting.

**Slide 6**
Good to know
It stains everything it touches, so work with care. As a culinary spice it is
enjoyed freely the world over. As a concentrated supplement it can interact with
blood thinning medication and is not for high doses in pregnancy, so ask your
practitioner.

## 02 · Ginger

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 02
**Ginger**
The universal remedy of both Ayurveda and TCM.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Ayurveda calls ginger vishwabhesaj, the universal medicine. TCM uses fresh
ginger to warm the middle and dispel cold. Modern study centres on its gingerols,
the compounds behind its warmth and its well documented ability to calm nausea
and support digestion.

**Slide 3**
Fresh and dried are different tools
Fresh ginger is bright, juicy and moving, best for cold season sniffles,
circulation and cooking. Dried, ground ginger is hotter and more concentrated,
warming from the inside, and a staple of baking and spice blends.

**Slide 4**
Bring it into the day
A few slices simmered for ten minutes with lemon and honey to wake up digestion.
Grated into stir fries and broths at the start. And kept close on travel or
queasy days, one of its best supported uses.

**Slide 5**
Good to know
Ginger is warming and moving, so very large amounts can feel intense if you
already run hot or have reflux. In concentrated or supplement form it can
interact with blood thinning medication, so have a word with your practitioner
if that is you.

## 03 · Cinnamon

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 03
**Cinnamon**
Warmth you can taste, and a gentle friend to blood sugar.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
In Ayurveda and TCM, cinnamon is used to warm the body, support circulation and
settle digestion. Its best known modern claim is a gentle role in steadying blood
sugar levels after meals, which is why it pairs so naturally with sweet foods.

**Slide 3**
Ceylon or cassia?
Ceylon, or true cinnamon, is lighter, more delicate and low in coumarin. Cassia
is the darker, cheaper, stronger cinnamon most common in supermarkets, but higher
in coumarin, which is not ideal for the liver in large daily amounts. For everyday
sprinkling, either is fine. If you use it heavily, choose Ceylon.

**Slide 4**
Bring it into the day
A dash over oats, stewed apple or pear, which also blunts the sugar spike. A stick
simmered into tea, cocoa or golden milk. And quietly savoury too, in stews,
tagines and garam masala.

**Slide 5**
Good to know
As a spice, cinnamon is a delight. As a concentrated daily supplement, especially
cassia, moderation matters because of coumarin. Enjoy it the way kitchens always
have, generously, but as seasoning.

## 04 · Cardamom

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 04
**Cardamom**
The fragrant queen of spices.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Cardamom is prized across Ayurveda, TCM and Middle Eastern medicine for its
bright, aromatic warmth. Ayurveda considers it tridoshic, balancing to all
constitutions in moderation, and reaches for it to lift heaviness, freshen the
breath and ease bloating.

**Slide 3**
Green and black
Green cardamom is the familiar sweet, floral pod, for chai, baking and desserts.
Black cardamom is smoky and bold, a savoury spice for stews, dals and rice. Buy
whole pods where you can, ground cardamom loses its fragrance quickly.

**Slide 4**
Bring it into the day
Crush two or three green pods into simmering masala chai with ginger, cinnamon
and cloves, the classic after meal digestive. A pod or a pinch in the coffee pot,
the Arabic way. And a pod dropped into basmati as it cooks.

**Slide 5**
Good to know
A little goes a long way, cardamom is intense, and one or two pods is usually
plenty for a pot. It is gentle and widely enjoyed as a spice, and as always,
concentrated extracts are a different matter from the pinch in your chai.

## 05 · Cumin

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 05
**Cumin**
A cornerstone of the Indian spice box.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Cumin, or jeera, is one of the most used spices in Ayurvedic cooking, prized for
kindling agni, the digestive fire, and for easing bloating and heaviness after
meals. Warm, earthy and a little nutty, it forms the base of countless spice
blends.

**Slide 3**
Get the most from it
Toast whole seeds until fragrant to deepen the flavour and, traditionally, its
digestive power. Whole seeds bloom in hot oil at the start of cooking, ground
cumin stirs in later. Its natural partners are coriander and turmeric.

**Slide 4**
Bring it into the day
Jeera water: a teaspoon of seeds simmered in water and sipped warm, a simple
after meal digestive. Or CCF tea, equal parts cumin, coriander and fennel seeds
steeped in hot water. A tempering of seeds sizzled in ghee to start a dal. A pinch
through rice, roasted vegetables or a cooling raita.

**Slide 5**
Good to know
Cumin is gentle and enjoyed daily across the world as food. As always,
concentrated supplements are a different matter from the spice, so check with your
practitioner before taking them.

## 06 · Black Pepper

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 06
**Black Pepper**
The king of spices, and the key that unlocks turmeric.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Black pepper, or kali mirch, is one of the oldest traded spices, valued in
Ayurveda for stimulating digestion and clearing cold and damp from the body. Its
bite comes from piperine, which modern research shows can dramatically increase
how much your body absorbs from other foods.

**Slide 3**
The turmeric connection
Even a pinch of pepper alongside turmeric meaningfully boosts how much curcumin
you absorb. The two belong together, in warm milk, in a curry, in a pinch.

**Slide 4**
Get the most from it
Grind it fresh, its aromatic oils fade quickly once ground. Add it late, piperine
is delicate, so a final grind toward the end of cooking keeps its punch.

**Slide 5**
Bring it into the day
The classic trio of turmeric, black pepper and a little fat. A finishing grind
over eggs, soups, salads and roasted vegetables. And trikatu, where pepper joins
long pepper and ginger in a warming Ayurvedic digestive blend.

**Slide 6**
Dishes to try (all veg)
Black pepper rasam, a thin, peppery South Indian broth to reach for at the first
sign of a cold. Cacio e pepe, the Roman pasta built on almost nothing but pepper
and cheese, easily made vegan. And milagu kuzhambu, a dark, savoury pepper and
tamarind gravy over rice.

**Slide 7**
Good to know
As a kitchen spice, pepper is enjoyed freely. In large medicinal or supplement
doses, piperine can change how the body processes certain medications, so check
with your practitioner if that applies to you.

## 07 · Fennel

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 07
**Fennel**
The sweet, cooling seeds offered at the end of a meal.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Fennel, or saunf, is the gentle, sweet, cooling member of the spice box. Ayurveda
considers it balancing to all three doshas and one of the best spices for calm
digestion, easing bloating and cramping without heating the body.

**Slide 3**
Why it appears after dinner
That little dish of fennel seeds at the end of an Indian meal is not just for the
breath. Fennel settles the stomach and cools the system, the perfect close to a
rich meal.

**Slide 4**
What gives it its power
Fennel's sweet liquorice note comes from anethole, the compound behind its
carminative, or gas relieving, action: it relaxes the gut and releases the trapped
wind behind bloating. It cools rather than heats, so Ayurveda reaches for it with
acidity and heartburn, and it has long been offered to nursing mothers.

**Slide 5**
Bring it into the day
Chew a pinch of seeds, plain or toasted, after eating. Steep them into a calming
tea for bloating and a restless tummy. Or make CCF tea: equal parts cumin,
coriander and fennel seeds, the classic Ayurvedic digestive blend. And cook with
them, lovely with roasted vegetables and potatoes, folded into breads, or stirred
through lentils and dals.

**Slide 6**
Good to know
Fennel is gentle and widely enjoyed. Concentrated fennel extracts are much
stronger and, as with any supplement, worth checking with your practitioner,
particularly during pregnancy.

## 08 · Mustard

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 08
**Mustard**
Pungent and warming in every form, from seed to oil to greens.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Few spices are as versatile as mustard. Warming and sharply pungent, it turns up
as seeds, as oil, and as leafy greens, and each part carries its own character.
It is a winter crop, and across northern and eastern India the cold months bring
both the seeds and the greens into their own.
Also known as sarson, rai, sarshapa, shorshe and sorisha.

**Slide 3**
The many forms
Black and brown seeds, the pungent everyday cooking seed. Yellow seeds, milder,
the base of pastes and pickles. Mustard oil, golden and warming, a defining
flavour of Bengali and North Indian cooking. And mustard greens, the iconic
sarson ka saag of Punjab.

**Slide 4**
A note from the kitchen
Bengali cooking leans on mustard beautifully. Where much of India reaches for
whole and ground spices, Bengali kitchens often work with spice pastes, and a
ground mustard seed paste coats vegetables evenly and gently, without the constant
stirring that knocks delicate veg about.

**Slide 5**
Traditional dishes to try
Sarson ka saag from Punjab, slow cooked greens with makki di roti. Kasundi from
Bengal, a pungent fermented mustard relish. Shorshe aloo from Bengal, potatoes in
a bright mustard seed sauce.

**Slide 6**
Good to know
Mustard is warming and pungent, so a little goes a long way, and raw mustard paste
is especially fiery. Enjoyed as food it is a joy, and as with any spice,
concentrated extracts are a different matter.

## 09 · Bay Leaves

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 09
**Bay Leaves**
The quiet backbone of the pot.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Bay leaves work in the background, but you notice when they are missing. Added
whole to a simmering pot, they lend a soft, sweetly aromatic warmth that rounds
out rice, dals and slow cooked dishes. In Ayurveda they are valued for gently
supporting digestion.
Also known as tej patta.

**Slide 3**
Indian and Mediterranean bay are different
The bay leaf of Indian cooking, tej patta, comes from the cassia family and
smells warmly of cinnamon and clove. The Mediterranean bay of a European stew is
a different leaf altogether, more herbal and eucalyptus like. Recipes usually mean
one or the other.

**Slide 4**
Choosing, keeping and using
Buy whole dried leaves and store them airtight, away from light. Add them whole
and early so they have time to infuse. Then remove before serving, bay leaves
flavour the pot but are not meant to be eaten.

**Slide 5**
Traditional dishes to try
Pulao and biryani, where a leaf or two perfumes the rice. Dal and slow cooked
curries, infused from the start. And garam masala, the warming blend that often
includes tej patta.

## 10 · Curry Leaves

**Slide 1 (cover)**
KNOW YOUR SPICES / 10
**Curry Leaves**
The soul of South Indian tempering.

**Slide 2**
Why it earns its place
Curry leaves quietly define a whole cuisine. Tossed fresh into hot oil, they
crackle and release a warm, citrus nutty aroma that is the signature of South
Indian home cooking. Traditionally they are valued for supporting digestion, and
even for hair and skin.
Also known as kadi patta.

**Slide 3**
Fresh is everything
Use them fresh, dried leaves lose most of their magic. Store fresh sprigs wrapped
in the fridge, or freeze them, and they keep for weeks. And a note on the name:
curry leaves have nothing to do with curry powder, they are the leaf of a specific
tree.

**Slide 4**
Cooking with them
Their home is the tadka, or tempering: a spoon of hot oil or ghee, a scatter of
mustard seeds, and a handful of curry leaves thrown in to sizzle for a few
seconds before the lot is stirred through a dish. Unlike bay leaves, they are soft
enough to eat and are usually left in.

**Slide 5**
Traditional dishes to try
Sambar and rasam, the comforting soups of a South Indian meal. Coconut chutney
and lemon rice, both lifted by a curry leaf tempering. And poriyal, simple stir
fried vegetables finished with mustard seeds and curry leaves.
