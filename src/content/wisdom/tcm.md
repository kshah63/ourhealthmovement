## Health as flow, not parts

Where modern medicine often zooms in, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) zooms out. It
asks how the whole system is *moving*: is energy flowing or stuck, is the body too warm or
too cold, too damp or too dry? Symptoms are read as patterns of imbalance rather than
isolated faults, and the aim is to restore flow.

### Yin, yang and qi

At its heart are two ideas. **Qi** (pronounced *chee*) is the vital current that moves
through *meridians*, the channels acupuncture and acupressure work with. **Yin and yang**
are the paired qualities (cooling/warming, still/active, receptive/expressive) whose
balance defines health. Nothing is purely one or the other; the goal is a supple,
responsive equilibrium.

### The five elements and the seasons

TCM organises the world into five elements, each tied to a season, an organ network and
an emotion:

- **Wood** (spring) · the Liver · growth, planning, and gentle detox after winter.
- **Fire** (summer) · the Heart · joy, connection, and cooling foods.
- **Earth** (late summer) · the Spleen · nourishment, digestion, and warm, easy meals.
- **Metal** (autumn) · the Lungs · letting go, and moistening foods as the air dries.
- **Water** (winter) · the Kidneys · rest, reserves, and deep, warming stews.

This is why a TCM kitchen changes with the calendar: warming, drying foods in damp
weather; cooling, hydrating foods in the heat. It's seasonal eating with a 2,000-year-old
logic behind it.

### Ancient patterns, modern curiosity

Acupuncture, tai chi and qi gong, all rooted in this tradition, are now studied for
their effects on pain, stress and the nervous system. You don't need needles to begin,
though: eating with the season, and moving gently every day, are TCM's most accessible
prescriptions.
